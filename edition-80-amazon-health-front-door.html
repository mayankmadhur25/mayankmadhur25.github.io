Amazon built a front door to American healthcare. Who will build India's?

In eight years, Amazon has bought three healthcare companies, launched and shut several of its own experiments, and slowly joined the pieces into one route: find care on the Amazon app, see a doctor, get the prescription delivered the same day. It is trying to own the front door to American healthcare.

India has its own race for that front door, and Amazon is already in it: Amazon Medical in India now offers medicines, online doctor consultations and home lab tests in one app. Apollo, Tata, PharmEasy, Reliance and Flipkart hold other pieces, and the government is building a public version through ABHA and UHI. Amazon's US path shows what it takes. India's version will look different, because Indian healthcare is local.

In short

  1. Amazon bought what was hard to build (a licensed pharmacy, a primary care network) and built the rest around it.
  2. It shut what did not work: Haven, Amazon Care and the Halo wearable. Healthcare stayed a priority; individual products did not.
  3. By 2026 the pieces connect: an AI health assistant on the Amazon app, One Medical doctors, and same-day prescription delivery in a growing list of US cities.
  4. In India, Amazon is copying the loop without buying clinics: pharmacy, online doctors and home lab tests through a partner. Apollo is the only player that owns the whole chain.
  5. About 43% of treated illnesses in India are handled by private local doctors and clinics. India's front door will be a national app over local providers, shaped by e-pharmacy rules still in draft since 2018 and a public route the government is building.

Amazon's eight years: buy, build, close

Amazon did not buy dozens of health companies. It made three healthcare acquisitions, built products around them, and closed experiments that did not work.

Deal values as disclosed: PillPack as cash paid net of cash acquired; One Medical including net debt. Health Navigator's and Bee's prices were not disclosed.

The most telling move is the switch from Amazon Care to One Medical. Amazon built its own virtual care service, offered it to employers, and shut it at the end of 2022 because it was not complete enough for large customers. A few months later it paid about $3.9 billion for One Medical, which already had clinics, doctors, employer contracts and a working mix of in-person and online care.

The front door Amazon is building

By 2026 the parts connect into one loop, with Prime as the way in.

Each step is now in place. One Medical membership costs Prime members $9 a month. The Health AI assistant moved from One Medical members to the Amazon app in 2026, where it can explain results, book visits and help with medicines. Amazon Pharmacy plans same-day delivery to nearly 4,500 US cities and towns by the end of 2026, and One Medical patients in Los Angeles can collect prescriptions from kiosks in clinic lobbies before they leave.

Three lessons from Amazon

1

Buy the regulated, hard part

Pharmacy licences across 50 states and a working primary care network take years to build. Amazon bought both and built the consumer layer itself.

2

Close what does not work, keep the goal

Haven, Amazon Care and Halo all closed. Each closure was followed by a different route to the same place.

3

Distribution is the advantage

Prime and the Amazon app bring patients in cheaply. Delivery logistics turn a prescription into medicine in hours.

There is a fourth point that matters as much: regulation follows. When the One Medical deal closed, the US Federal Trade Commission warned that misusing patients' health data could break the law, and Oregon approved it only with five years of reporting. Owning the front door means owning the trust problem too.

India's race for the front door

In India, several groups are assembling the same loop, mostly starting from the pharmacy.

My reading of each group's public offering in October 2026. Service lists change; check each company before quoting.

Amazon's Indian version is already more than a pharmacy. In 2025 it put three services under one Amazon Medical section of its app: Amazon Pharmacy, which it says now reaches all its serviceable PIN codes; Amazon Clinic, online consultations with licensed doctors; and Amazon Diagnostics, home lab tests run with Orange Health Labs in six cities, with samples collected within an hour. Amazon's own venture fund had invested in Orange Health a few months earlier. What Amazon India does not have is a clinic. In the US it bought one. In India, it has not tried.

Apollo is the only player that owns every piece: hospitals, clinics, a pharmacy chain, diagnostics and an app. Tata 1mg and PharmEasy combine pharmacy with lab tests, the latter after buying Thyrocare. Reliance and Flipkart are mostly pharmacy and delivery.

Indian healthcare is local, so the front door will be too

The biggest difference between India and the US is where people actually get treated. In the government's 2025 health survey, about 43% of illnesses treated outside hospitals were treated by private doctors and clinics, in villages and cities alike. Most of these are neighbourhood practices, not chains. Medicines often come from the chemist down the road, whom many people trust more than an app.

So the Indian front door is unlikely to be one company's owned chain, as in the US. It is more likely to be a national app that finds, books and pays, sitting on top of local doctors, chemists, lab technicians and nurses who do the work. Amazon India's choices already point that way: licensed partner sellers for medicines, a partner lab for tests, online doctors for the first consult. Its quick-delivery network of small neighbourhood warehouses could help too, within the limits that pharmacy rules set on how medicines are stored and handed over.

Why India's front door will look different

1

The pharmacy rules are still a draft

India published draft rules for online pharmacies in August 2018. They were never made final. On 20 May 2026, the chemists' body AIOCD, which says it represents over 12 lakh chemists, called a nationwide shutdown demanding the withdrawal of the 2018 draft and of a Covid-era home-delivery notification. Amazon's US pharmacy rests on licences in all 50 states. India's online pharmacies rest on rules that have been pending for eight years.

2

Most care is paid out of pocket

Households paid 43.4% of India's health spending directly in 2022-23. There is no employer-paid primary care membership at scale for a One Medical to sell to. People compare prices visit by visit, which suits pay-per-use and Prime-style bundles more than a costly membership.

3

The government is building its own front door

ABHA, the UHI booking network, eSanjeevani teleconsultations and Jan Aushadhi stores are a public version of the same loop. As Edition 77 and Edition 79 showed, it works best where the government runs the service, and private players have been slow to join. India's front door may end up as public rails with private apps on top.

What it means

1

For Amazon and other platforms

Buying a national clinic chain, as Amazon did in the US, fits India poorly. The better route is to become the trusted app that routes patients to good local doctors, labs and chemists, and to be neutral about it. A platform that steers every patient to its own services will lose the trust it needs.

2

For hospital groups and clinics

Apollo shows the value of owning care and pharmacy together. Others will have to decide which platforms to sit behind, and on what terms for patient data, referrals and fees.

3

For the government

Finalise the online pharmacy rules, so patients and honest players are not left in a grey zone. And bring private platforms onto UHI, so the public rails and the private apps connect.

4

For investors

Count connections, not deals: how many users move from app to doctor to test to medicine and back. Amazon's record also shows that a closed product is not an exit from healthcare.

What to watch

  • Whether the government finalises India's online pharmacy rules, or withdraws the 2018 draft as chemists demand.
  • Whether Amazon Diagnostics grows beyond its six cities, and whether Amazon Clinic adds in-person care through partners.
  • Any acquisition of a clinic or primary care chain by Tata, Reliance or Flipkart.
  • Whether any private health app joins UHI as a full participant.
