One autistic life, six systems, and where India's support runs out

India identifies autism in childhood, but support has to last a lifetime. This edition follows one autistic person through home, school, college, hospital, work and adult life, and shows what each place can change, from the clinic waiting room to the job interview.

Last week, Edition 81 set out what Autism For Dummies (Wiley, 2025) teaches. Four ideas matter most here: autism lasts a lifetime; behaviour is a message about what a person is going through; surroundings can disable or enable; and many adjustments are simple. This edition tests what India does with them. The book is about the US and the West, so the Indian analysis and proposals here are mine.

In short

  1. India's formal autism tools and most visible services are built around children. An autistic person then meets six systems across a lifetime, and the support gets thinner at each one.
  2. From first signs to first job, most of the adjusting is done by autistic people and their families. India could move support to the moments when life changes: early checks, a transition plan at 14, life skills, college and job support, and planning for life after parents.
  3. A 2018 five-region study estimated autism in about 1% of children aged 2 to 9. India has no national estimate for adults.
  4. Diagnosis and disability certification are different steps. Families can get stuck at stigma, distance and paperwork.
  5. Schools, hospitals and employers can each make low-cost changes now. The outpatient visit is the easy case; emergency and inpatient care are harder.
  6. One practical idea ties it together: a one-page communication passport that travels with the person from hospital to school to work.

One autistic person, six systems, one lifetime MY ANALYSIS

India can notice, diagnose and certify an autistic child. But the child grows up. They move from home to school, college, hospital visits, work and adult life, and at each step they meet a new system with its own rules. In my assessment, India's formal tools and much of its visible service infrastructure are child-focused, and support gets thinner at every later step.

My analysis. The fixes are examples, not a complete list.

Growing up autistic in India: from first signs to first job MY ANALYSISMY PROPOSAL

The six systems above are places. A life is a sequence. Here is how it typically unfolds, how autistic people and their families adjust at each stage, and one new step India could take at each.

A typical path, drawn from the book's chapters on each life stage and my reading of Indian conditions. Real lives vary widely; many autistic people need far more or far less support than shown.

Read down the middle column and a pattern appears. At almost every stage, the adjusting is done by the autistic person and their family. Children mask to get through school. A parent, usually the mother, cuts back work to manage therapies and admissions. Teenagers are pushed to fit in just as anxiety rises. Young adults hide their diagnosis at college and at work, and many burn out. And parents carry one fear above all: what happens when they are no longer there. The book's chapters on teenagers, higher education, work and complex needs describe each of these strains; in India, with fewer services, they fall even more heavily on families.

The right-hand column is what changes that. Most of it is not new money for new buildings. It is moving support to the points where life changes:

1

Catch it early, without blame

Use existing child health contacts, such as vaccination visits and school health checks, to spot developmental differences and refer quickly, with clear information that autism is nobody's fault.

2

Plan transitions before they happen

From age 14, a simple written plan, reviewed each year with the young person, family and school, covering what comes after school: further study, skills training or work.

3

Teach the skills adult life needs

Money, travel, cooking, health, body changes and how to say no. The book argues these matter as much as academics for a good adult life.

4

Support at college and the first job

A named support contact in every college, and job coaches who help with hiring and the first months at work. Supported employment, where a coach works alongside the new employee and the manager, is one of the book's recommended routes.

5

Plan for life after parents

Supported living options, short breaks for carers, and help with financial and legal planning, so families do not face the future alone.

What India knows, and what it does not INDIA DATA

A 2018 population study led by the INCLEN Trust, across five regions, estimated autism in roughly 1% of children aged 2 to 9, and found that nearly 1 in 8 had at least one neurodevelopmental condition. It is the best community estimate India has, but it is not national, it is eight years old, and it covers only children.

The route from noticing to support INDIA DATA

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 lists autism spectrum disorder among 21 recognised disabilities. A person assessed at 40% or more counts as having a "benchmark disability", which opens access to education support, job reservation and scheme benefits. Getting there takes six steps.

The six steps follow the government's assessment and certification process. The points where families can get stuck are my reading, drawing on the book's chapter on diagnostic barriers.

One distinction is often blurred. A clinical diagnosis tells a family what is going on and guides care. Disability certification, using the Indian Scale for Assessment of Autism and leading to a UDID card, decides eligibility for particular legal benefits. A family may need the first without wanting the second, or need the second to unlock help at school or work. Clinics should explain both.

What an autism-ready hospital visit looks like MY PROPOSAL

The book's chapter for medical professionals asks doctors to share decisions with patients, adapt how they communicate, adjust the sensory environment and treat parents as partners. Start with the building itself. Here is what an autism-ready clinic could look like, and why each feature is there.

An illustrative floor plan, not a real hospital. Orange numbers match the table below.

What it isWhy it is there
1. Picture signs at the entranceShows where to go without asking. Unclear directions and having to ask strangers add stress before the visit even starts.
2. Registration without the queueA small desk in a clear entrance lobby, with QR registration from outside or a family member registering. The registration queue is often the loudest, most crowded point of the visit.
3. A quiet waiting cornerScreened, with softer light and away from the TV and loudspeaker. Long waits in noise are a common trigger for overload.
4. Wait outside, get a callAn SMS or phone call when it is nearly their turn, so the patient can wait in a car, garden or corridor. Names shouted over a loudspeaker are hard to process.
5. Consultation rooms set up for calmSoft or dimmable light instead of flickering tube lights, a door that closes, a choice of chair, and picture or communication cards on hand. The patient can take part in the consultation instead of coping with the room.
6. Picture steps in the procedure roomA simple card showing each step of a blood test or injection, shown before touching. Unexplained touch and sudden pain are frequent causes of distress.
7. A calm roomA small, quiet room to step into during a meltdown or shutdown. Overload passes faster with less noise and fewer people watching.
8. Toilets with paper towelsLoud hand dryers and echoing tiles are among the most commonly reported sensory triggers. A quieter option costs almost nothing.
9. A pharmacy on the way outPlaced off the corridor near the exit, not in the entrance lobby, so arriving patients are not caught in the medicine queue. It gives written and picture instructions: spoken ones, given once in a busy queue, are easy to miss.

My proposal, drawing on the book's chapters on sensory experiences and on service providers. Most items need no construction: a screen, a sign, a phone call, a laminated card.

The space is half of it. The other half is how each step of the visit is run.

My application of the book's guidance for medical professionals and its sensory and communication chapters to a typical Indian OPD.

The simplest change comes before the visit: record, once and with consent, how the patient communicates, what overwhelms them, how they usually show pain and who supports them. A single field in the hospital record would spare families from repeating it at every visit.

The OPD is the easy case. A scheduled visit can be made predictable. Emergency care brings alarms, long and uncertain waits, unfamiliar staff, pain and urgent procedures. A hospital stay brings broken sleep, changing nurses, new food and new routines. Hospitals should carry what they know about the patient into the emergency room and the ward, instead of asking the family to start again.

A communication passport MY PROPOSAL

One idea ties the school, hospital and work changes together: a one-page communication passport, kept by the person or family and shared with consent. It answers the questions every new teacher, doctor or manager would otherwise ask, or never ask.

My proposal. The idea exists in some health systems abroad; the fields here are illustrative.

India already has the plumbing to make this digital. A passport could sit in a patient's health record app, linked to their ABHA, and be shared with a hospital or school only when the person agrees. That fits the consent-based records system covered in Edition 79.

Work: getting in, and staying MY PROPOSAL

The RPwD framework makes reasonable accommodation a core right and sets duties on non-discrimination in employment. The practical problem is turning that principle into everyday hiring and management. The book's workplace chapters suggest most adjustments are cheap.

My grouping of adjustments described in the book's workplace chapters.

The interview can become the first major barrier. Conventional interviews reward quick social responses, eye contact and small talk, which may have little to do with the job. A short task-based assessment, questions sent in advance and a quiet room change who gets through the door.

The other half is job design. Instead of asking how an autistic employee can cope with a job, ask which parts of the job best match their strengths. For some people that will be sustained focus, spotting patterns, checking quality or deep knowledge of one area. Not every autistic person has these strengths, so ask the person. For India's IT services firms and global capability centres, which hire at scale and already run diversity programmes, this is a practical opening.

What India should do MY PROPOSAL

1

Make hospitals and clinics autism-ready

Start with the low-cost OPD changes, carry patient preferences into emergency and inpatient care, and add them to hospital accreditation standards and medical and nursing training.

2

Build routes for adults

Create diagnosis pathways and tools tested with adults, including women and people who mask, and bring certification closer to where people live. Build supported decision-making wherever possible, and reserve substitute decision-making for situations where it is genuinely necessary.

3

Train the front line for the right job

Train ASHA and anganwadi workers to recognise developmental concerns, talk about them without stigma and know where to refer, not to make the diagnosis themselves. Train teachers, OPD staff and police to tell overload from defiance.

4

Pilot a communication passport

Start in a few district hospitals and schools, on paper and linked to the health record with consent, and see whether families and staff use it.

5

Count adults, and include autistic people

Add autistic adults to national surveys, publish certificate data by age, and put autistic people on the committees that set the rules.

India does not need a separate autism system for every part of life. It needs ordinary systems that stop assuming everyone communicates, senses, learns and works in the same way.

The test is not how many children India identifies. It is whether an autistic person can move from school to college, to work, to a hospital bed and into adult life without falling off the system each time.
