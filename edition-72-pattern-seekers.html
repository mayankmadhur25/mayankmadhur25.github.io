Two boys who wouldn't talk

Two boysThe engineThe caveThe genesThe puzzleWhy it happensEindhovenWhat holds upWhere I stopThe fieldThe spectrumMy viewFour wordsJonahIndiaTakeawaysSources

Simon Baron-Cohen's The Pattern Seekers opens with two small boys, and refuses to tell you who they are. Both are late to talk. Both question everything, sort everything, repeat experiments, love rules, ignore what other children do, have few friends. One of them holds 1,093 US patents. The other is unemployed and lives with his parents. Nothing in the shared list explains the difference in those two lives.

What this edition is
  • Reading notes on one book and one finding inside it: the wiring that makes humans inventors overlaps with the wiring behind autism.
  • The strong claims hold up. Autistic people do score higher on pattern-seeking, tested across 671,606 people, and the genetic overlap with autism is real at 26 per cent of common variants.
  • The popular version does not hold up. The book never says autism is the next stage of evolution, and there is no evidence for it.
  • Four places where I stop agreeing, including the one that matters most: only the articulate, high-IQ success stories are in the book.
  • For India, the same pattern I keep finding: prevalence is measured, entitlements are published, and employment has no national figure at all.

The first boy is Al. Slow to start talking, then never stops asking questions. A teacher calls his brain addled. The second boy is Jonah, two years old, also not talking. His mother is frightened. The paediatrician draws a chart of how children learn to speak, marks an X below the lowest line, and tells her she has seen many children sitting right there. Some became engineers. Some became artists.

Be proud of Jonah. He's just on a different track. And if other parents ask why he's not talking yet, just say, he's different, but not worse.

The paediatrician, chapter one

Jonah starts talking just before three, and does it oddly. He points at things and names them, not to show anyone, just for himself. He spends hours flicking one switch at the top of the stairs while leaving every other switch in the house up, checking, again and again, which light it controls.

Al turns out to be Thomas Alva Edison. Jonah grows into a man who drives to the coast every weekend and can read a shoal of fish through the ripples on the surface. When Baron-Cohen calls the other fishermen his friends, Jonah corrects him.

They like me because I show them where the fish are. But after the fishing trips they go to the pub, and I go home alone, and still live with my parents.

Jonah, chapter one

Jonah is autistic. You are told at the end of the chapter, by which point the argument no longer needs making.

02 — The argument

The book is not really about autism

I picked it up expecting a book about autism and it is a book about invention. Autism is the evidence.

The question is one I had not thought about: how does anything get invented? Not who invented what, but what the mind is doing at that moment. Baron-Cohen's answer is that there is one engine and it does one thing. It looks for if-and-then patterns.

If I put a seed in the ground, and it rains, then a plant grows. If I make a hole in this bone, and cover the hole and blow, then the note changes. If I press this switch, and the wiring runs to that lamp, then the light goes on. The engine finds the rule, then varies one part of it and checks what happens. That last step, systematic variation, is the whole thing.

Which reframes the boy on the stairs. A four-year-old flicking a light switch three hundred times is either a symptom to be managed or an experiment being run three hundred times. The behaviour is identical. What changes is what you think is happening.

03 — The cave

The best part of the book has nothing to do with autism

Baron-Cohen goes to the Hohle Fels cave in southern Germany, where the oldest musical instrument ever found was recovered, a flute made from bone. An archaeologist points at a layer in the rock wall: this line is twenty thousand years ago. This one is forty.

Somebody down there picked up a hollow bone and wondered what sound it made. Covered the hole. Uncovered it. Drilled a second hole and started again.

The timing matters because of what came before. For roughly three million years, stone tools barely change. Then, somewhere between 100,000 and 70,000 years ago, everything changes at once: bows and arrows, shell beads, cave art, the flute.

Figure 3
Three million years of almost nothing, then everything at once
How complex human tools got over time. Log scale, so the right-hand side is compressed.

The counting argument is the clearest thing in the book. A spear with a stone tip takes six steps, and they have to happen in a workable order. Six steps can be arranged 720 ways, and almost all of them produce nothing. Getting from one working sequence to a longer working sequence is not trial and error at random. It is a mind holding a rule and changing one variable at a time.

04 — The genes

The part I could actually check

Most evolutionary psychology stops once the story sounds good. This book does not, which is why I kept reading.

Working with 23andMe, the Cambridge team looked at the DNA of people who had taken empathy and systemizing tests. Fifty thousand had taken the systemizing test. Three separate genes came back linked to it. Twin studies put around 28 per cent of the differences in empathy down to inheritance. Under 5 per cent of autism comes from rare mutations, which means the overwhelming majority comes from many common variants each with a tiny effect.

Then the question the whole book depends on. Do the variants linked to strong systemizing overlap with the variants linked to autism?

Figure 4
Shared genes, and not the same thing
Common gene variants linked to strong systemizing, against those linked to autism.
26%of common gene variants linked to strong systemizing line up with variants linked to autism. Later work sharpens it further: the overlap sits on the repetitive and detail-focused side of autism, not the social side.

That last detail is the one that gets lost, and it is the one that matters. The genetic story connects pattern-seeking to the repetitive traits, not to the social difficulties. Which means the popular summary, autistic people are natural engineers, is doing something the evidence does not support.

05 — The puzzle

So why is autism still here?

Put the pieces together and something does not add up. Autism runs strongly in families, so much of it is inherited. It also comes with much lower rates of having a partner, holding a job and living independently. In plain evolutionary terms, a trait like that should thin out over generations.

It has not. WHO puts autism at about 1 in 127 people worldwide in 2021. A global review lands near 1 per cent. US surveillance finds about 1 in 31 among eight-year-olds.

That gap, between what simple evolution would predict and what we actually observe, is the interesting part of this book. It is also the part people skip when they say autism is the next stage of evolution.

Three possible answers, all testable, none settled. One, the effects are tiny: about 95 per cent of autism comes from many common genes, each so small that selection barely sees it. Two, a useful trade-off: if empathy and systemizing trade off against each other, the useful middle of the curve carries the tail along with it. Three, like marries like: put strong systemizers in one city, they pair up, and the tail gets thicker within a generation.

The third answer is the only one with evidence behind it, and it makes a testable prediction about geography. If strong systemizers cluster in particular places, meet each other and have children, then autism should be measurably more common in those places. That is the claim the next section examines, and it is where the book goes looking.

06 — The evidence on cause

What the data actually says about why autism happens

The book is about invention, so it treats causation lightly. But the argument only works if the genetics hold, and this is a subject where the popular version and the evidence have drifted a long way apart. So here is what the data supports, and what it rules out.

Strongly supported
It is largely inherited
Twin and family studies consistently find autism among the most heritable of neurodevelopmental conditions. Siblings of autistic children have a markedly raised likelihood compared with the general population.
Strongly supported
Many small genes, not one big one
About 95 per cent of the genetic contribution comes from many common variants, each with an effect so small that natural selection barely registers it. Under 5 per cent comes from rare mutations. There is no single autism gene.
Strongly supported
It overlaps with pattern-seeking, partly
26 per cent of common variants linked to strong systemizing overlap with variants linked to autism, and the overlap sits on the repetitive and detail-focused traits rather than the social ones.
Associated, not proven causal
Some pregnancy and birth factors
Higher parental age, very premature birth and low birth weight all show statistical associations in large cohort studies. Association is not mechanism, and none of these explains more than a small share.
Ruled out
Vaccines do not cause autism
Tested repeatedly across millions of children in multiple countries, including a Danish cohort of over 650,000. The original 1998 paper was retracted and its author struck off the UK medical register. This is one of the most thoroughly investigated questions in modern epidemiology, and the answer has not changed.
Ruled out
Parenting does not cause it
The mid-century "refrigerator mother" theory, that cold parenting caused autism, was abandoned decades ago. It caused real harm to a generation of families and has no evidential basis.

The honest summary is uncomfortable for everyone. Autism is mostly inherited, through hundreds of common gene variants that each do almost nothing on their own, interacting in ways nobody can yet trace from gene to trait. We can say with confidence what does not cause it. We cannot yet say, for any individual child, what did.

Which is also why the evolutionary question in this edition is genuinely open rather than settled. A trait with hundreds of tiny genetic contributors behaves very differently under selection than a trait with one big one.

07 — The test

Eindhoven, where you can watch it happen

This is the assortative mating hypothesis, which is a formal way of saying that people tend to pair up with people like themselves. Eindhoven is the technology capital of the Netherlands, built around its technical university and a century of Philips. If strong systemizers move to technology cities and have children with each other, recorded autism should be higher there than in comparable Dutch cities.

09 — The caveats

Where I stopped agreeing

Four things worth holding onto, because the book is convincing enough that you might slide past them.

Caveat 01
The "male brain" idea is shaky
The male and female differences come mostly from questionnaires people fill in about themselves, which is exactly where social expectation leaks into the data. You can discard this part of the theory entirely and the rest of the book still stands.
Caveat 02
The 70,000-year date is slipping
A 2026 paper in Nature Communications describes the Xigou site in central China, where people were making planned stone tools and fitting them to handles far earlier than the cognitive revolution window allows. If the date moves, the story around it has to move too.
Caveat 03
One number is doing too much work
A 26 per cent overlap in common gene variants is a real finding. It is a long way from the version that reaches a policy note. The damage happens in the summarising, not in the study.
Caveat 04
Only the success stories are in the book
Every figure held up as an example speaks well and has a high IQ. Across global studies, roughly a third of autistic people also have an intellectual disability, and many are non-speaking. A theory of invention built on the most articulate slice of a population tells you very little about the rest of it.

The fourth is the one I would press hardest. A theory of human invention built on the most articulate slice of a population tells you very little about the rest of it, and it creates a specific risk: that autistic people become interesting to policymakers only insofar as they are useful. That is a bad reason to support anyone.

10 — The wider field

What the rest of the research actually thinks

Baron-Cohen's is one theory among several, and reading only his book gives a misleading sense of consensus. So before my own view, here is the field: where it agrees, where it splits, and which theories autistic researchers themselves have put forward.

Where there is broad agreement

Settled
It is strongly heritable
A meta-analysis of twin studies puts heritability at 64 to 91 per cent, and estimates of 70 to 90 per cent are routinely cited. This has held up across decades and multiple methods.
Settled
Prevalence around 1 to 2 per cent
With a male-to-female ratio commonly reported near 4 to 1, though how much of that ratio is biology and how much is diagnostic bias is actively disputed.
Settled
No single autism gene
Many genes are implicated. Common variants contribute substantially to heritability; rare and de novo gene-disrupting variants account for a minority. Polygenic risk scores predict only weakly at the individual level.
Emerging
It may not be one condition
A 2025 study from Princeton and the Simons Foundation used data from over 5,000 children to identify four biologically distinct subtypes, each with different traits, developmental paths and genetic signatures. If that holds, "autism" may be a label sitting on several different things.

Where the field splits

Baron-Cohen's position
Empathizing–Systemizing, and the extreme male brain
Autistic people show below-average empathizing alongside intact or superior systemizing, which he framed in 2002 as an extreme of the typical male profile. The SQ and EQ questionnaires operationalise it, and The Pattern Seekers extends systemizing into a theory of human invention.
The main objections
Stereotype in, stereotype out
Critics argue the male-female contrast rests on self-report questionnaires, exactly where social expectation contaminates the measure. The stronger charge is practical: it helped produce a diagnostic picture modelled on a boy with technical interests, so women, non-binary people and anyone who masks were systematically missed. A 2021 critical-neurodiversity paper went further, drawing an uncomfortable parallel with Galton's habit of theorising from "talented men at Cambridge". Autistic critics also note that autistic people who do not systemise simply do not appear in the theory at all.

The theories autistic researchers built instead

Damian Milton, 2012
The double empathy problem
Social difficulty is not a deficit sitting inside the autistic person. It is a mutual failure of understanding between people with different ways of processing the world. The evidence is that autistic people communicate well with other autistic people, and non-autistic people with each other; the breakdown happens across the two groups. That reframes the whole question of whose deficit it is.
Murray, Lesser and Lawson, 2005
Monotropism
Attention is the unit, not empathy. Autistic minds tend toward a single-minded, deep focus on whatever holds interest, with real difficulty pulling away from it. It was the first theory of autism built from autistic subjective experience rather than from observed behaviour, and it accounts for the sensory aspects the older cognitive theories left out.

There is also Uta Frith's weak central coherence account, that autistic cognition favours local detail over global context, which can sit alongside the systemizing account rather than against it. And the broader neurodiversity position holds that disability emerges from the interaction between a person and their environment, which makes interventions aimed at normalising autistic behaviour both ineffective and, in several studies, harmful to mental health.

And one finding that should make everyone cautious about prevalence numbers. A forensic analysis of reported autism across 206 countries from 1990 to 2019 found that inflows of official development aid predicted rising reported cases, with the effect strongest in low-income countries with weaker health systems and government effectiveness. The authors are careful: genuine increases and diagnostic inflation leave the same statistical footprint, so this cannot settle which is happening. But it means cross-country prevalence comparisons carry a funding signal as well as a health signal, which is worth remembering before treating any national figure as a measure of the underlying trait.

What this changes about the book. Baron-Cohen's theory is genuinely productive and it is genuinely contested, and both of those are true at once. The invention argument does not depend on the male brain claim, which is why I said earlier you can discard that part and keep the rest. But the double empathy problem does something the systemizing account cannot: it explains why the same person can be socially fluent in one room and lost in another. Any serious reading of this subject has to hold both.

11 — The spectrum

What "spectrum" actually means, and who the book leaves out

The word spectrum gets used as though it means a line from mild to severe. It does not. The diagnostic manual assesses two things separately, and a person can sit at opposite ends of them.

Two consequences follow. A person described as "high functioning" may need very substantial support in one domain and almost none in another. And the level can move: it describes support needs now, not a fixed capacity.

On speech, and a correction worth making

There is a widespread impression that most autistic people do not speak. The research does not support that.

25–30%of autistic children do not develop functional speech and remain minimally verbal beyond about age five. It is a substantial minority, not a majority. Around 30 per cent of autistic adults are minimally verbal.

The terms matter. Minimally verbal usually means a small number of spoken words or fixed phrases. Non-speaking or non-verbal means little or no speech. Neither describes intelligence, and neither is necessarily permanent: one longitudinal study found roughly 30 per cent of children who were non-speaking at age two had developed phrase or fluent speech by eight.

Why speech does not develop, when it does not

Motor
The mouth, not the mind
Childhood apraxia of speech and related motor conditions affect the physical production of speech. The intention to communicate is intact; the mechanism for producing sound is not.
Language processing
Different route to language
Differences in how spoken language is processed, including echolalia, where speech is repeated rather than generated. Brain imaging work finds music paired with words increases attention to speech, which is why music-assisted approaches are being trialled.
Co-occurring
Associated genetic conditions
In one clinical series, identifiable genetic syndromes were found in 22.6 per cent of minimally verbal autistic individuals, roughly twice the rate in the general autistic population.
Not the reason
It is not a measure of intelligence
Nonverbal IQ is lower on average in this group, but not uniformly: several studies find minimally verbal children with typical or borderline nonverbal IQ, which points to a genuine split between verbal and nonverbal functioning. Non-speaking is not non-thinking.

And this is where the book's blind spot has consequences. Almost all cognitive research on autism, including the pattern-seeking work, has been done with people who speak fluently and score well on verbal tests. Minimally verbal autistic people are roughly a quarter to a third of the population and a very small share of the literature.

So when a theory of human invention is built on systemizing scores from questionnaires, remember what a questionnaire requires. The people least represented in the evidence are the people whose support needs are greatest, and no evolutionary argument, mine included, should be allowed to obscure that.

12 — My own view

Why I think autism is an evolutionary story, and what that does not mean

Everything up to here has been the book and the evidence. This section is my own reading, and I want to separate it clearly from both.

Start with the deep timeline, because it changes the scale of the question. Anatomically modern humans have been around for roughly 300,000 years. For most of that, stone tools barely changed. Then, somewhere between 100,000 and 70,000 years ago, tools, ornaments, art and music appear across the record in a rush. Farming is about 12,000 years old. Writing is about 5,000. Almost everything we call civilisation sits in the last four per cent of our species' existence, and it all runs on the same cognitive machinery that was assembled long before any of it.

Against that scale, the framing of autism as a modern condition looks odd. The genes involved are common variants, present across populations, inherited through hundreds of small contributions. Those were not manufactured by modern life. They are old.

What I do think
  • Autism sits inside human evolutionary history, not outside it. A highly heritable trait carried on common variants across every population is part of the species' standing variation, not a deviation from it.
  • Its persistence is a genuine evolutionary puzzle. A trait this heritable, with these effects on partnership and independent living, should have thinned. It has not. That gap demands an explanation, and "modern diagnosis" does not cover it.
  • The most plausible answers are ordinary population genetics. Hundreds of variants with effects too small for selection to see; a trade-off where the useful middle of the curve carries the tail; and assortative mating thickening the tail locally. None is exotic and all are testable.
  • Pattern-seeking looks like something that was selected for. The counting argument about tool sequences is the strongest part of the book. A mind that holds a rule and varies one element at a time is a plausible target of selection, and autism shares some of its genetic basis.
What I do not think, and will not argue
  • Autism is not the "next stage" of anything. Evolution has no direction, no ladder and no next. That phrasing imports a Victorian idea of progress that biology abandoned a century ago.
  • Autistic people do not exist in order to invent things. A trait having consequences is not the same as a trait having a purpose. The moment you argue people exist for a function, you have made their standing conditional on their output.
  • Sharing genes with a useful trait is not a justification for anything. Support is owed on the basis of need. If the evolutionary argument turned out to be wrong tomorrow, not one person's entitlement should change.
  • The invention story does not describe most autistic people. Roughly a third also have an intellectual disability, and many are non-speaking. Any evolutionary account built only on the articulate, high-IQ cases is describing a slice and calling it the whole.

The distinction I care about is between two sentences that sound similar and are not.

“Autism is the next step in human evolution” is unfalsifiable, teleological and used mostly to flatter. “Autism is an outcome of human evolution, and its survival is a question worth answering” is a research programme with testable predictions. The first is a slogan. The second is why I found this book worth eleven thousand words.

And there is a practical reason the distinction matters beyond the science. If autism is framed as an upgrade, support looks optional. If it is framed as a disease, difference looks like damage. The evolutionary reading, done carefully, supports neither. It says this variation is old, it is part of us, it carries real costs to real people, and the costs are ours to address regardless of what the variation ever contributed to anyone's invention.

13 — The useful part

Four words, all true at once

The most useful thing in this book for policy is not the evolution story. It is a four-way split that ends the argument about whether autism is a difference or a disease. The answer is that it is all four, about different things, in the same person.

Difference
A different mix of strengths. Neither better nor worse.
Asks forRespect
Disability
Below average at a skill, and needs help in daily life.
Asks forSupport
Disorder
Something that hurts, and we do not yet know why.
Asks forResearch
Disease
Something that hurts, and we know why. Epilepsy, for instance.
Asks forTreatment

Difference or disease is a false choice. The useful part is the bottom row: each column asks for a different response, so a policy that picks one word and applies it to everything will be wrong three times out of four. You can throw out the male brain theory completely and still keep this frame, which is why it is the part of the book most likely to survive the arguments about it.

The fix already exists and it is not glamorous. Specialisterne in Denmark replaced the job interview with a task: build and program a Lego robot, and let the pattern-seeking speak for itself. The interview is a social performance test. For a large number of capable people it screens out exactly the wrong thing.

15 — India

Where India stops counting

India has the population, the tech hubs and the laws. What it does not have is the measurement in between. This is the same problem I keep finding across Indian health programmes: big ambition at the front, entitlements written down, and nothing recorded in the middle.

The measuring stops here

And India's own numbers cut against the tidy version of this story. The INCLEN survey found autism ranging from 0.4 per cent in urban North Goa to 1.8 per cent in rural Palwal, a four-and-a-half-fold gap running in the opposite direction to the Eindhoven finding. Rural, not urban. No technology hub involved.

The most likely explanation is not biology but method: different survey teams, different awareness, different thresholds for what gets recorded. Which is the same lesson as Eindhoven, arriving from the other side. When the measurement varies more than the trait plausibly does, you are measuring the measurement. Anyone planning to run the Eindhoven comparison across Bengaluru or Hyderabad should settle that first, or the result will be unreadable.

The measuring stops exactly where accountability would start. India can tell you how common autism is. It can tell you what an autistic person is entitled to. It cannot tell you how many autistic children who were screened went on to be diagnosed, or how many diagnosed adults are working. Around 1 in 8 Indian children carry at least one neurodevelopmental condition, and the pathway after screening is unrecorded.

Four moves. Two of them are cheap.

Low effort, high leverage
Instrument the pathway
Publish RBSK screening yield, DEIC referral completion and UDID issuance as one linked annual series. A reporting rule, not new money.
Low effort, high leverage
Fix the front door of hiring
Swap the interview for a work sample across GCC testing, data quality, coding and security roles. Specialisterne in Denmark did exactly this and it works.
Higher effort, worth commissioning
Watch the hubs
Run the Eindhoven comparison across Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune and Chennai. If assortative mating is real, India's tech cities are where it shows.
Higher effort, fund it anyway
Separate support from productivity
Fund Niramaya, Samarth and Gharaunda on need alone. The employment argument must never become the reason someone deserves support.

The two moves with the most impact are also the two cheapest, because both change a process rather than adding spending. Instrumenting the pathway costs a reporting rule. Replacing the interview with a work sample costs a hiring manager's afternoon.

16 — Takeaways

Key takeaways

01
The strong claim is true and narrower than the headline. Autistic people do score higher on pattern-seeking, tested across 671,606 people, and share 26 per cent of common gene variants with it. But that overlap sits on the repetitive traits, not the social ones, so "autistic people are natural engineers" is doing something the evidence does not support.
02
The book never claims autism is the next stage of evolution. That line comes from its readers. There is no evidence for it, and repeating it makes the real finding harder to defend.
03
Autism is mostly inherited through hundreds of tiny genetic effects. Roughly 95 per cent from common variants, under 5 per cent from rare mutations. Vaccines and parenting are ruled out. What caused it in any individual child remains untraceable.
04
The evolutionary puzzle is real and unresolved. A trait this heritable, with these outcomes, should thin out. It has not. Assortative mating is the explanation with actual evidence behind it, and Eindhoven is the test, though it measures diagnoses rather than autism.
05
Difference, disability, disorder and disease are all true at once, about different things. Each asks for something different: respect, support, research, treatment. A policy that picks one word and applies it to everything is wrong three times out of four. This survives even if the male brain theory is discarded entirely.
06
The employment gap is a sorting failure, not a welfare statistic. 62 per cent of autistic males are strong systemizers; 16 per cent are in full-time work. Specialisterne replaced the interview with a work sample, which tests the skill rather than the social performance.
07
India measures the beginning and the entitlement, and nothing in between. Prevalence is known at roughly 1 in 89. There is no national figure for how many diagnosed adults are employed. The measuring stops exactly where accountability would start.
08
This book is one theory in a contested field. Heritability at 64 to 91 per cent is settled. The extreme male brain framing is not, and it helped produce a diagnostic picture that missed women and anyone who masks. Autistic researchers built alternatives: the double empathy problem locates social difficulty between people rather than inside one of them, and monotropism starts from attention rather than empathy.
09
Roughly 25 to 30 per cent of autistic people are minimally verbal, not a majority, and speech is not a measure of intelligence. Reasons include motor speech conditions, language processing differences and co-occurring genetic syndromes. These are the people least represented in the research and most in need of support.
10
"An outcome of evolution" and "the next stage of evolution" are different sentences. The first is a testable research question. The second is a slogan that makes support look optional. I hold the first and reject the second.
11
The two highest-leverage fixes are the two cheapest. Publish the screening-to-diagnosis-to-entitlement pathway as one linked series, and replace the interview with a work sample. Both change a process rather than adding spending. And support must be funded on need alone, never on productivity.
