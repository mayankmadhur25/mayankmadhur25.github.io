India is adding hospital beds fast. The real battle is making each bed work.

ExpansionThe equationMaturity curveCorrelation testARPOBThroughputCapital eventsBuild vs buyMedicoverForecastWhat to measureMethods & sources

India's hospital sector is adding capacity at a pace that makes the ownership story only half the story. In Q1 FY27, hospitals under Kotak Institutional Equities' coverage reported 21% year-on-year revenue growth and 22% EBITDA growth, while operational beds increased 15% and capacity beds 16%. Aggregate EBITDA margin stayed around 23%. And ICRA expects the sector to add roughly 14,500 beds at a capital cost of ₹30,000 to 32,000 crore across FY26 and FY27. The question is no longer simply who owns the beds. It is how quickly each new or acquired bed becomes productive.

The thesis after re-testing the data
  • Occupancy matters inside a hospital because fixed costs are high, but cross-company occupancy does not reliably explain cross-company margins.
  • Revenue grew faster than bed count. The simple ratio says revenue per operational bed increased about 5.2% in Q1 FY27.
  • An occupied bed is not a standard economic unit. FY26 ARPOB among major chains ranges from roughly ₹44,640 to ₹77,800 in the comparable CRISIL table.
  • Length of stay matters. The sector's aggregate ALOS moved from 4.1 days in FY24 to 3.9 in FY26; at constant occupied-bed days that implies about 5.1% more patient episodes.
  • Capital changes scale faster than mature economics. The event study still shows revenue and bed bases moving far faster than margins.

02 — The equation

A hospital bed has four different ways to become more productive

For inpatient economics, the cleanest first-order identity is:

Revenue identity
Inpatient revenue ≈ Operational beds × 365 × Occupancy × ARPOB
ARPOB is average revenue per occupied bed per day, not revenue per operational bed. Specialty mix, payer mix, geography, technology and ALOS all influence the observed ARPOB.

The Q1 FY27 sector numbers allow a useful decomposition without pretending we know every hospital's occupancy:

Q1 FY27 decomposition
Revenue per operational bed growth = (1.21 ÷ 1.15) − 1 = 5.2%
Beds grew 15%; revenue grew 21%. Therefore the revenue/operational-bed ratio grew about 5.2%. That residual contains utilisation, ARPOB, mix and timing effects. It is not an attribution to one lever.

Capacity is expanding, but the economic value of capacity is expanding too. That is why bed count alone is an incomplete healthcare-growth measure.

03 — The maturity curve

A bed can exist clinically before it works economically

New hospitals and acquired hospitals enter a portfolio at different points on the maturity curve. Staff, equipment and depreciation arrive before utilisation is mature. The operating story is therefore sequential, not instantaneous.

ICRA's FY26 sample illustrates the mature-sector baseline: 18% revenue growth, 63.5% occupancy, 9.2% ARPOB growth and a 24.1% operating margin. For FY27 it expects 13–15% revenue growth, 6–8% ARPOB growth and 22–24% margins as new capacity partly offsets operating leverage.

04 — Re-testing the occupancy claim

The correlation does not survive a change of year

What these two numbers mean
Pearson r
Measures whether two numbers rise and fall together by matching amounts. +1 means they move together perfectly, 0 means no link, −1 means one rises as the other falls.
If fuller hospitals always earned proportionally wider margins, Pearson r would sit near +1.
Spearman ρ
Compares only the order. It ignores how big each gap is and checks whether the fullest hospital is also the most profitable, the second fullest the second most, and so on.
Useful here because hospital margins are reported on slightly different definitions, so the order is more trustworthy than the exact figures.
The 95% interval
The band the true figure most likely sits within. A narrow band means a solid finding. A range that stretches across zero means the direction itself is unresolved, because the data cannot even tell you which way the relationship points.
Both intervals below stretch across zero. With six companies, that is what you would expect.

Six companies is a small sample. The tests are still worth running because they discipline a claim, and worth reporting with their intervals because that is where the uncertainty shows.

The original edition used a seven-chain FY25 cross-section and found a negative occupancy–margin correlation. That was useful as a challenge to the simplistic story, but not strong enough to call occupancy the “wrong lever.” I re-ran the test on the same six companies for which CRISIL's FY25 and FY26 tables provide both occupancy and company-reported EBITDA margin.

The sign flips. In the same six chains, Pearson occupancy–margin correlation moves from about −0.30 in FY25 to +0.30 in FY26. Both confidence intervals are enormous and cross zero. The defensible conclusion is not that occupancy is bad or good. It is that a one-year cross-company correlation cannot identify the causal operating lever.

06 — The throughput lever

Occupancy can stay flat while a hospital treats more patients

Average length of stay (ALOS) changes the number of patient episodes a bed can support. CRISIL's aggregate sample moved from 4.1 days in FY24 to 3.9 days in FY25/FY26. At unchanged occupied-bed days, that alone increases potential episode throughput by about 5.1%.

Throughput identity
Annual inpatient episodes ≈ Beds × 365 × Occupancy ÷ ALOS
A lower ALOS is not automatically “better” clinically; it only becomes an efficiency gain when read alongside outcomes, readmissions and case mix.

6,779
At 3.5 days: +14.3% throughput
7,908
At 3.0 days: +33.3% throughput

The three-year aggregate picture is revealing: occupancy moved 64.7% → 64.8% → 62.8%, ARPOB moved ₹54.7k → ₹58.0k → ₹61.7k, and ALOS moved 4.1 → 3.9 → 3.9 days. A simple available-bed-day revenue proxy — occupancy × ARPOB — therefore rose from ₹35.4k to ₹38.7k, a 9.5% increase even though occupancy finished lower.

The “available-bed-day revenue proxy” is an analytical product of two aggregate metrics, not a company-reported KPI. Because the peer aggregates can use different weighting bases, use it directionally, not as audited revenue per bed.

08 — Build versus buy

India is doing both — and the distinction matters

ICRA expects a sample of 18 large hospital chains to add 34,000+ beds during FY26–FY30, a cumulative 48–50% increase over their March 2025 capacity, with an indicated outlay of about ₹40,000 crore. A simple division is roughly ₹1.18 crore of announced outlay per planned bed, but this is not a construction-cost benchmark because the portfolio mixes brownfield, greenfield, land, equipment and timing.

09 — The live test

What KKR actually bought, in management's own words

The deal is often described as a bet on a broken asset bought cheap. Medicover's own executive director told Reuters something rather different, and the detail changes the story.

The stated reason for seeking a buyer

The last card is the one that changes the story. Management said the decision to talk to private equity came because the debt position was rising and becoming difficult to sustain. This was a balance-sheet event before it was a growth story. That is a materially different transaction from the one implied by the headline multiple.

And 19 of 25 hospitals are already profitable. So the buyer is not fixing a failing chain. The work is refinancing an over-levered balance sheet and ramping the six hospitals that have not matured yet, which is exactly the pattern the maturity curve in section 03 describes.

The utilisation gap is nonetheless real, and larger than a first pass suggests. On 6,000 beds, moving from roughly 45 per cent occupancy to the stated target of 4,000 occupied beds is an increase of about 1,300 occupied beds a day, close to 48 per cent more occupied capacity, with the stated intention of converting capacity beds into operational and chargeable ones rather than building new hospitals.

Where the numbers disagree, and it matters. Analyst commentary around the deal put the FY25 EBITDA margin near 11.8 per cent on revenue of about $217 million. Management subsequently described the current margin as 14 per cent. Those are probably an audited full-year figure and a current run-rate, but they are not the same number and neither is an audited disclosure available publicly. Treat both as indicative.

10 — Forecast

A driver-based forecast is more honest than an “advanced” time-series model here

There are not enough consistent annual observations — and there are too many structural breaks from acquisitions and new beds — to justify ARIMA, machine learning or a high-order regression. A transparent driver-based scenario is more defensible. I use ICRA's published FY27/FY28 revenue and margin ranges, with FY26 revenue indexed to 100 and FY26 operating margin fixed at the reported 24.1% starting point.

Forecast mechanics
Revenuet+1 = Revenuet × (1 + growth)
EBITDA index = Revenue index × scenario margin ÷ FY26 margin
This is a scenario model, not a prediction interval. It shows the earnings consequence of ICRA's own published ranges.

11 — What to measure after the deal

Five numbers tell us whether a bed is actually working

1

Same-hospital occupancy

Separate mature hospitals from newly commissioned and acquired units. A blended network average hides ramp-up.

2

Like-for-like ARPOB

Report the existing estate separately from acquired hospitals. Manipal and Max show why acquisition mix can mechanically dilute group ARPOB.

3

ALOS and patient throughput

Occupancy without length of stay cannot tell whether a bed serves more patients. Pair ALOS with readmissions and outcomes so efficiency does not become premature discharge.

4

Licensed, capacity, operational, chargeable and occupied beds

Medicover's 4,800-versus-6,000 disclosure illustrates the problem. The denominator changes the occupancy story.

5

EBITDA per bed and new beds versus acquired beds

One measures productivity; the other separates healthcare capacity creation from ownership transfer.

India's next hospital race will not be decided by who announces the most beds.It will be decided by who can turn each bed into more access, more throughput and sustainable clinical economics.
