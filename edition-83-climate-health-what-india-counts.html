Climate is reaching India's hospitals. The health system counts only part of it.

Climate change is already reaching India's hospitals. It comes as heat, floods, mosquitoes, dirty water, smoke, poor harvests and lost homes. The 2026 Lancet Countdown says half of the heatwave days Indians faced in the last ten years were caused by climate change. COP31 starts in Antalya this week. For Indian healthcare, the real question is simple: how much of this harm can our own health system actually see?

Heat is the area India measures best. In 2024, the system confirmed 161 heatstroke deaths and 48,156 suspected cases. But on an average day, only 55% of reporting units sent data. A study of just ten cities estimates about 1,116 heatwave deaths a year in those cities alone. If this is the best-measured area, the others are weaker still.

In short

  1. Climate reaches our health in eight ways: heat, severe weather, air pollution, mosquitoes, pollen, water, food and work, and displacement. The chain and the wheel at the top show how each one ends up at a hospital.
  2. Heat is measured best. Surveillance confirmed 161 heatstroke deaths in 2024. A study of ten cities estimates about 1,116 heatwave deaths a year in those cities alone. They measure different things, and that gap is the story.
  3. Only 55% of 47,477 reporting units sent heat data on an average day in 2024, up from 23% in 2023. Almost half were still silent.
  4. In NCDC's 2024 check of 5,690 facilities, 99% had ORS corners, but only 32% had emergency cooling ready. Prevention is common. Rescue is not.
  5. Every district was told to finish a climate-health plan by the end of 2025. There is still no official count of how many did.
  6. Beyond heat, the counting gets thinner. No national figure was found for hospitals shut by floods, for pollen, for heat illness at work, or for climate-linked mental illness.

The whole chain: from climate to the hospital

Community medicine explains climate and health as a chain. Climate changes the world around people. Those changes reach the body as illness. This edition adds two more links that matter for anyone running healthcare: the pressure this illness puts on hospitals, and what the system must be ready with before the next season.

Heat as the test: three ways of counting deaths

Heat is where India has the most data. There is national surveillance, a hospital readiness check and good research on deaths. So heat is the fairest place to check how well the health system sees climate harm. Most of us read a heat death number as a full body count. It is not. Each number below answers a different question, in a different place, using a different method. Read them side by side. Never add them up.

Sources: NCDC, Report of Heat-Health Preparedness and Response Activities 2024; Rajya Sabha Unstarred Question 1693, 6 August 2024; de Bont et al., Environment International 184, 2024.

The surveillance number is narrow on purpose. It counts only heatstroke that a reporting hospital saw and confirmed. The research number is broad on purpose. It counts every extra death on hot days, whatever the death certificate says. Both are useful. Only one of them guides the health system today.

NCDC's own 2024 report says it clearly: heatstroke mostly happens outside hospitals. So a count made inside hospitals will always catch only a small part of it.

Almost half the health facilities did not report

Before asking how many died, ask who reported. India's heat surveillance moved to the Integrated Health Information Platform in 2023. Every public facility from the primary health centre upwards is supposed to report daily. In 2024, on an average day, only a little over half did.

Source: NCDC, Report of Heat-Health Preparedness and Response Activities 2024, section 5 (reporting period 1 March to 31 July).

Reporting more than doubled in one year. That is real progress. But it also means part of any rise in reported cases simply comes from more hospitals reporting. NCDC itself says year-to-year numbers must be read along with how many units reported. Until reporting becomes steady, a rising count cannot tell us whether heat illness itself went up.

Three of these six steps are leaks: the death may never enter the count at all. Two are delays: timely cooling could have saved the patient. Only the last step gives the number the country sees. The system is well designed. The coverage is weak.

Ready on paper: what the 2024 hospital check found

In summer 2024, NCDC asked health facilities to check their own heat preparedness. 5,690 facilities replied, and 69% of them were primary health centres. The pattern is clear. The easy and cheap steps are in place almost everywhere. The step that actually saves a heatstroke patient is missing in most places.

Source: NCDC, Report of Heat-Health Preparedness and Response Activities 2024, section 7 (assessments 12 April to 31 July 2024).

ORS prevents dehydration. But it cannot save a patient whose body temperature has reached 41°C. That patient needs a rectal thermometer, ice, a cooling bath or body bag, a fan, and staff who have done this before. The guidance already exists: NCDC's emergency cooling guidelines list what each level of facility should keep. Even the programme's own target is modest: a working heat stroke room in 30% of district and sub-divisional hospitals in heat-prone states in 2026–27, and 40% the year after.

The district plan that was due by end of 2025

India plans for climate and health at three levels. A national action plan. A state plan for every state and union territory; all 36 are now on NCDC's website. And a district plan, which is the one that names the hospitals, the budget and the people responsible.

NCDC's district guidance for 2026–28 is very clear: every district should have finished its District Action Plan on Climate Change and Human Health by the end of 2025, and the target for 2026–27 is 100%. Its 2024 report said many districts were still drafting. Today there is no official public count of how many districts have finished. One number is going around from a test version of the national climate dashboard, but it does not appear on any government website, so I have not used it.

One more point. NDMA reports Heat Action Plans in 23 states, 195 districts and 64 cities. These are disaster-management plans, run by a different ministry. They help, but they are not the health department's plan for heatstroke rooms, surveillance and cooling.

What a climate-ready district should prove

The chain at the top of this edition ends with five things a health system needs: emergency readiness, surveillance linked to weather forecasts, strong facilities, continuity of care and district planning. Each of these can be tested.

The plans are on paper. Now we need proof that they work on the hottest day of the year and in the worst week of the monsoon. Here are six tests, and each one can be measured:

1

Report the reporting

With every heat, dengue or respiratory bulletin, publish how many facilities reported that week. A case count without this is not a trend.

2

Count deaths the way researchers do

Use the civil registration system to track total deaths against temperature, and after floods, district by district, along with confirmed heatstroke. The ten-city study shows this works with records India already keeps.

3

Link forecasts to surveillance

Heavy rain should start a watch for dengue and diarrhoea in the weeks after. Poor air quality should start a respiratory watch. A heat alert should get heatstroke rooms ready. Publish which alert led to which action. And investigate every suspected heat death through the verbal autopsy route that already exists.

4

Drill before each season

Every February, run a timed cooling drill in each heat stroke room and ambulance. Before every monsoon, run a continuity drill in flood-prone districts: power backup, water, drug stores above flood level, and how staff will reach the hospital. Publish the results.

5

Publish district plan status by name

Put the list of districts that have finished their climate-health plan on an official website, with dates. The deadline was the end of 2025.

6

Measure whether hospitals stay open

Add one simple indicator: hours of service each district hospital lost to floods, cyclones, power cuts or heat, and how many dialysis, delivery and chemotherapy appointments were missed. Without this number, there is no baseline for spending on resilience.

In three years, India has built more of a climate-health system than in the ten years before. Heat surveillance on a national platform. Cooling guidelines. Heat stroke rooms. CHEST clinics. A plan in every state. The next step is less exciting but more important: counting properly across all eight areas, and publishing what the count shows.

India now gives heat, rain and air-quality warnings well. But its count of the harm is partial, and for most areas it hardly exists. Closing this gap is the preparedness work still left, and every district can start before next summer and next monsoon.
