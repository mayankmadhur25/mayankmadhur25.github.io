Autism is in our classrooms and offices. Most of us still don't see it.

Ask ten people what autism is. Most will describe a child who does not talk. Some will mention a film. Very few will think of a colleague, a cousin or the quiet student in the last bench. That picture is the problem. About 1 in 100 children in a 2018 Indian study. Hundreds of linked genes. No national count of autistic adults. We still know little about autism, and most people do not know it is already around them.

I spent the last few weeks with Autism For Dummies (Wiley, 2025). 25 chapters. Three authors: John Marble, who is autistic, and Khushboo Chabria and Ranga Jayaraman, whose son was diagnosed at college. It is written for families, doctors, teachers and employers. It changed how I see a few things. This edition covers what the book teaches. Next week covers what India should do about it.

In short

  1. The book's core idea is simple. Autism is a lifelong difference in how people think and sense. It is not an illness to cure. Much of the difficulty comes from places built without autistic people in mind.
  2. Behaviour carries information. Meltdowns and shutdowns are overload. They are not defiance.
  3. Masking gets people through the day, but it has a cost. It is one reason girls, women and adults are missed.
  4. For doctors, the details matter. Vaccines and parenting do not cause autism. Other conditions are common. Pain can show up differently.
  5. Many adjustments cost little: clear words, warning before change, a quiet space, patience. But some autistic people need serious long-term support.

Six lessons from the whole book MY ANALYSIS

Read the 25 chapters together and six ideas keep coming back.

1

Change the surroundings, not only the person

Many difficulties start in places that are too loud, too bright, too rushed or too unpredictable. Changing the place is often the fastest help.

2

Behaviour is a message

Behaviour carries information. Pain, overload, fear, confusion or an unmet need. Ask what it is saying before correcting it.

3

Plan for a whole life

Autistic children grow up. School, college, work, relationships, health care and ageing all need planning, not only the early years.

4

Start from strengths

Focus, honesty, attention to detail and deep interests count at school and at work. Support works better when it starts from them.

5

Ask autistic people

The best guide to what helps is the person, or the people who know them best. Services designed without them usually miss.

6

Many useful adjustments are ordinary and inexpensive

Clear words, warning before changes, a quiet space and patience cost little. Every institution can start tomorrow. But some autistic people need substantial long-term support too: communication devices, therapy, supported living.

Three ways to see autism BOOK SAYSMY ANALYSIS

Most of us were taught one view of autism: a list of deficits to correct. The book leans the other way. It sees a different way of being that the world should adjust to. It also says plainly that the difficulties are real. Read as a whole, its advice lands in the middle. Autism can be a natural difference and a real disability at the same time. Support the person, and remove the barriers.

This changes what happens in a clinic. A child covers their ears in a noisy waiting room. One view calls that difficult behaviour. The others call it a loud room, and maybe a child who needs tools for noise. Therapy still matters. Its purpose changes.

Autism is a profile, not a scale BOOK SAYS

Most people picture autism as a line from "mild" to "severe". The book shows why that misleads. A person can speak fluently and hold a demanding job, and still struggle badly with noise, planning or sudden change. A person who does not speak may understand far more than anyone assumes. Needs also shift with age, place and stress.

Autism is not the same as intellectual disability. Autism affects communication, social interaction, sensory processing, patterns of behaviour and how information is processed. Intellectual disability means significant limits in reasoning and everyday adaptive skills. The two can occur together, but one does not imply the other.

A label would get both people wrong. Person A gets called "barely autistic" and gets no help with the noise and planning that exhaust them. Person B gets assumed not to understand, and follows everything.

Planning and organising deserves its own line. The book calls it executive function. Planning. Starting tasks. Managing time. Switching between activities. Adapting when plans change. Many autistic people find parts of it hard, even when they are very capable elsewhere. It often gets read as laziness. Visual schedules, small steps and warning before change help. "Try harder" does not.

Communication is more than speech BOOK SAYS

We tend to equate speaking with understanding. The book separates the two. Some autistic people talk a great deal. Some never speak. Many speak on some days and not on others, especially under stress. Every form of communication counts.

My summary of the book's guidance on communication, from its chapters on children, adults and media.

Conversations between autistic and non-autistic people often go wrong, and we usually blame the autistic person. The researcher Damian Milton calls it the double empathy problem. Each side follows different social rules. Autistic people tend to be direct, which others read as rude. Others rely on hints and tone, which autistic people can miss. The misunderstanding runs both ways.

Ten ideas from the book BOOK SAYS

The book closes with ten core ideas. Here they are in short form.

Teal: how to see autistic people. Blue: how autistic people experience the world. Amber: what helps them cope. My grouping and wording, based on the book's closing chapter.

Two matter more in India. Stimming, repetitive movement or sound, is usually treated as something to stop, at home, in school and in clinics. The book sees it as how a person calms an overloaded body. Leave it alone unless it causes harm. And "every autistic person is different" has a practical meaning. There is no single autism-friendly fix. Ask the person, or the people who know them.

Autism and human history BOOK SAYS

A theory worth knowing, briefly. The book's second chapter suggests that traits such as close focus and pattern-spotting may have helped early human groups track animals, read the weather and spot danger, and that autistic people were later pushed to the margins as societies became more rigid. It points to present-day examples such as Temple Grandin, the autistic animal scientist whose designs changed how livestock are handled. The early-history part is a hypothesis that cannot be tested directly; I covered the fuller version, Simon Baron-Cohen's The Pattern Seekers, in Edition 69. Respect and support should never depend on being exceptional.

What overload looks like BOOK SAYS

For anyone who deals with the public, the chapter on stress is the most useful in the book. Sounds, light, touch, crowds and sudden demands can overwhelm. Stress builds. Past a limit, the body reacts in one of three ways.

The middle bar is a stress meter. Each trigger adds to it: green is calm, orange is stress building, red is past the person's limit. Once it crosses the dashed line, one of three reactions follows. They are different kinds of reaction, not levels of severity: red for a loud, visible meltdown; grey for a quiet shutdown that is easy to miss; dark blue for burnout, which builds over weeks or months. My summary of the book's chapters on stress, meltdowns, shutdowns and burnout.

Most of us have seen a child melt down in a hospital or a shop and assumed bad behaviour. A tantrum and a meltdown can look the same from outside. But a meltdown is involuntary. Reasoning, punishment, raised voices and more demands make it worse. Less noise and more space help it pass.

Masking, and one reason people are missed BOOK SAYS

Many autistic people learn to hide their traits. They force eye contact. They copy expressions. They hold back stimming. They rehearse conversations. The book calls this masking. It gets people through the day, but it has a cost. It is also one reason autism is missed, along with stereotypes, low awareness among doctors, cost, distance, other conditions and cultural assumptions.

What masking can look likeWhat it can cost
Forcing eye contactAttention goes into looking, not listening; conversations become exhausting.
Holding back stimmingThe body loses a way to calm itself, so stress builds faster.
Copying others' expressions and phrasesA sense of not being able to be yourself; uncertainty about identity.
Rehearsing conversations in advanceConstant mental effort that leaves little energy for the day.
Saying "I'm fine" when overloadedOthers see no need, so support is withheld; anxiety and burnout follow.

My summary of the book's description of masking and its costs.

Telling people to stop masking does not work. Making places safe enough that they do not need to does. When autistic people can be themselves and still be accepted, needs become visible, and support can reach them.

This fits India closely. Autistic traits get mistaken for culture, or for the strain of poverty. In a crowded government OPD, a quiet child who avoids eye contact gets called shy, or well-behaved. A teenager who struggles with change gets called stubborn. Each is a missed diagnosis.

Where autism comes from: genes and other factors BOOK SAYS

Every parent asks what caused it. The book's answer is honest. Nobody knows the full story yet. Autism is rooted mainly in genetics. Other biological and developmental factors shape how it shows up. Nothing the parents did caused it.

The book borrows a comparison from the researcher Sue Fletcher-Watson. Genes shape autism the way they shape height. There is no single tall gene. Hundreds of genes each add a small push. Autism works the same way, which is one reason no two autistic people are alike.

Four kinds of genetic difference

My summary of the book's explanation. The gene names are examples the book gives; scientists have linked hundreds of genes to autism.

Two things follow. Many of these differences are inherited, so autistic traits often run in families. Some parents recognise themselves during their child's assessment, and seek a diagnosis of their own. But some changes are new in the child, so a family with no history can still have an autistic child. Neither is anyone's fault.

Switches, dials and the rest of the body

Having a gene is only part of it. The book compares genes to an instruction manual. The body also decides how the manual gets used.

My summary of the book's explanation of gene expression, regulation and interaction, and of other biological factors.

The book is careful about the bottom row. Gut, immune system, development and surroundings can shape how traits are felt, for better or worse. They do not cause autism. A calm place helps a person build on strengths. A stressful one makes communication and sensory control harder. The book also warns about devices and treatments sold as autism therapies with no proof behind them.

In practice: autism is diagnosed by watching development and behaviour, not by one gene test. Parents should not be blamed. Relatives may share some traits. Any diet, device or detox that promises to remove autism deserves deep suspicion.

Twelve details that matter for health care BOOK SAYSINDIA NOTE

Beyond the big ideas, the book has specific points every doctor, nurse and hospital manager should know. Here are twelve, and what each means in India.

What the book saysWhat it means in India
1. Autism is not caused by vaccines, parenting, diet or screensMothers are often blamed, and vaccine fears are real. Doctors should say this plainly at diagnosis.
2. Other conditions are common: anxiety, ADHD, epilepsy, sleep problems, motor and gut problemsTreat the whole person, and do not put every symptom down to autism.
3. Body signals can be felt late or shown differently: hunger, the need for the toilet, heat or cold, painAsk about pain in more than one way. A sudden change in behaviour can be pain.
4. Self-injury can signal pain or overload; stimming is usually harmlessLook for the cause of self-injury; leave harmless stimming alone.
5. Overload is often misread as defiance, and autism is sometimes labelled as something elseWhen a "behaviour problem" label does not fit, ask for a fresh assessment.
6. Misunderstanding runs both ways: different social rules on each sideStaff training should cover how staff come across, not only how patients behave.
7. Not speaking does not mean not understandingTalk to the patient, not about them; allow typing, pictures or a device.
8. Support levels at diagnosis are a snapshotNeeds change with age and setting; review them rather than fixing them for life.
9. Supported decision-making is better than full guardianship for most adultsIndia has legal guardianship under the National Trust Act and limited guardianship under the RPwD Act. Joint decisions should be the default.
10. Some children wander from safe placesHospitals, schools and events need a simple plan for a child who walks away.
11. Care works best when doctors, therapists and school share one planFamilies in India often carry the plan themselves. One named coordinator helps.
12. Be wary of anyone promising a cure, or therapy aimed only at making a child complyFamilies under pressure are easy targets. Doctors should help them judge claims.
