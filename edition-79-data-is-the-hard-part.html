The hard part of India's digital health: the problem, and five fixes

India has created 97.8 crore digital health IDs and linked 121 crore records. Those totals do not tell us how many patients have a medical history another doctor can actually use. India has built much of the national system. The next challenge is getting hospitals and clinics to create reliable, reusable data as part of everyday care, and that takes time, staff, trust and a reason to do the work.

In Edition 61, I showed that India's record totals hide how little is actually used. In Edition 41, I argued that hospitals lacked the infrastructure. This edition asks the next question: where digital health has worked, why it worked, and what that teaches about the rest.

In short

  1. The national system is largely built: 97.8 crore health IDs and 121 crore linked records. Its parts are at different stages of use.
  2. QR registration at hospital counters worked, and kept growing after its incentive stopped, because the data is simple and the benefit is instant.
  3. It stalls where the data is complex and the benefit comes later: clinical records and insurance claims.
  4. The people who must create good data gain least from it. The people who pay need it most.
  5. Five proposals: a small standard record, free conversion tools, payment for sharing, help at the counter that leaves patients in control, and public numbers on real use.

The system is built

Five years in, most of the national layer that only the government could build is in place, though its parts are at very different stages of use. What is missing sits mostly inside hospitals and clinics.

What worked: the hospital queue

Since 2022, patients at many government hospitals scan a QR code at the registration desk and get a token instead of queueing. A government-cited study found it cut OPD registration waits from about an hour to two to five minutes. Incentives for the service stopped in November 2025, and use kept rising. That does not prove payments played no part earlier, but the service clearly runs on its own now.

Where it stalls

Move from registration to records, and people drop out at every step.

Bar lengths are to scale within each group. ABHA linkage connects a visit to a patient's ID; it is not the same as a clinical record. Using ABDM software does not by itself mean records are shared.

Patients and devices are part of it, not only data. In Agra, 60% of 425 patients surveyed hesitated to share an OTP, and only 55% were comfortable with how their data was protected. At the eastern India hospital, poor phone signal and patients without smartphones made up about 72% of the recorded hurdles to ABHA linkage, and patient refusal another 22%. Hardware and connectivity still matter at the counter, even where the national system works.

That needs saying, because in Edition 41 I called infrastructure the biggest constraint. I would put it differently now. Phones, signal and computers are still a real barrier at the counter. But Scan and Share runs on the same phones, signal and hospital computers, and it works. For records and claims, the bigger gap is the data and the incentives behind it.

The big totals hide this, as Edition 61 showed: no official breakdown separates programme records, such as vaccinations, from notes written at a visit, and the dashboard still does not show how many records are actually shared between hospitals. On claims, the exchange carried 8.89 crore claim messages for PM-JAY and state schemes but only 30,246 for private insurance by September, according to the NHA dashboard I captured for Edition 76. Messages are not the same as settled claims.

Who does the work, who gets the benefit

NHCX claim messages since launch, by payer type, NHA dashboard, week of 21 September 2026. Government schemes include PM-JAY and state schemes. Message counts show where the channel is used; they do not prove the data inside is cleaner.

The players who pay need clean data most. IRDAI requires insurers to decide cashless requests within one hour, and that is far harder to do, and to check for fraud, when claims arrive as scanned PDFs. The people who create the data gain least. One likely reason PM-JAY's channel is busier is that the scheme both pays and runs the system hospitals use; private insurers pay but have less say over hospital software.

From easy to hard

My judgement, not a measurement: bar length reflects how much data a task needs and how late the benefit usually arrives. Some tasks, such as a fast cashless approval, can also pay off immediately.

India has done well at the top. The national totals mix all seven rows, so they make the hard rows look further along than they are.

Five fixes I would propose

1

A small standard record first (NHA, with hospitals)

A short fixed list every visit must produce: who the patient is, diagnosis, allergies, medicines, key results. Europe's International Patient Summary works this way.

2

Free tools to convert old data (NHA, software vendors, insurers)

Most hospitals will not replace their software. Give them free tools that convert what they already hold, the way Scan and Share plugged into existing systems, with clinical checks so nothing is guessed. Insurers and TPAs should accept claims from these tools in one format.

3

Keep paying for sharing, and show the results (NHA)

The most recently verified rates (April to September 2026) paid ₹10 to a hospital that requests a record with consent and ₹5 to the one that sends it. Renew that, pay for records that save a repeat test or a claim query rather than for volume alone, and publish how many payments were made.

4

Help at the counter (hospitals, states)

Design for elderly patients, shared phones and weak signal. Put staff or kiosks at registration to guide people through the steps, while patients keep control of their own OTP and consent. Staff should never collect a patient's OTP.

5

Count use, not sign-ups (NHA, IRDAI)

Publish the use measures I set out in Edition 61, such as records actually fetched across hospitals, and ask insurers, through IRDAI, to report what share of their cashless claims arrive in structured form.

What to watch

  • Whether NHA publishes records shared, separate from records loaded in bulk.
  • Whether the incentive scheme is renewed after September 2026.
  • Whether Scan and Share spreads to private hospitals.
