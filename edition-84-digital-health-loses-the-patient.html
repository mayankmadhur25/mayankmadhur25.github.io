EDITION 84 FRI 20 NOV 2026 Digital Health 12 MIN READ

In public hospitals, digital health follows the patient to the doctor. Then it starts losing them.

Earlier this year, a patient was reportedly turned away from a Delhi government hospital's ICU even though the online portal showed a free bed. The Delhi High Court ordered a surprise audit. NIC's teams visited all 38 Delhi government hospitals, which all run the same hospital software. 18 were using more than ten of its parts (modules), such as registration, lab and pharmacy. 20 were using only five to ten.

Same software. Same city. Very different use. The same pattern shows up across India: the digital record follows the patient to the registration desk and usually to the doctor. Then it starts losing them: at the pharmacy, inside the bigger hospitals, and on the next visit.

In short

  1. Registration is nearly solved. Bihar says about 92% of its government OPD registrations are online.
  2. The record thins out at the pharmacy. In 13 Tamil Nadu health centres, only 59% of prescriptions with medicines had a dispensing entry.
  3. In Kerala's Malappuram district, the typical government health centre enters about 99% of consultations in the system. The district hospital enters 4%.
  4. The next visit is the weakest step. Only about a third of Malappuram visits are linked to the patient's own ID.
  5. All of this is about government facilities. No private hospital publishes how its systems are used.
  6. No state can be ranked yet, because almost none publishes how the system is used. Tap any state below to see what each has shown.

One thing to know before reading: everything in this edition is about government health centres and hospitals. They are the only ones whose use of digital systems has been measured anywhere. Private hospitals are covered at the end.

How far the digital record follows the patient

Think of one visit to a government health centre. You register. The doctor sees you and writes a prescription. You give a sample at the lab. You collect medicines at the pharmacy. A month later, you come back. A digital system is useful only if it follows you through all five steps.

No state publishes all five for the same patients. But the best public evidence for each step tells a clear story.

Five steps, five different measures from three states. Read each bar on its own; they are not one group of patients. *Bihar's figure is the state government's own statement. The front of the journey is strong. Registration and the doctor's prescription are mostly digital wherever anyone has checked. The back of the journey is weak. The pharmacy and the return visit are where the record breaks, and these are the two steps that matter most for safety and continuity of care.

Only four states have shown how much the system is actually used: Kerala and Uttar Pradesh through their own dashboards, Tamil Nadu and Gujarat through published studies. Eleven say the software is installed widely but show no use data. Eight have specific problems found by national review teams, auditors or a court. Thirteen have too little evidence to judge.

Kerala: inside one district

Kerala does something no other state does at this scale. Every month, it publishes how each government health facility uses its eHealth system: how many patient visits were registered, how many consultations the doctor entered, and how many medicine and lab orders went through the system. Across the state, in February 2026, the doctor's consultation screen was in regular use in 89% of 708 government facilities, the medicine order screen in 84% and the lab order screen in 81%.

To see what this looks like on the ground, zoom into one district: Malappuram, in north Kerala.

Malappuram's February report lists every government health centre and hospital in the district on eHealth. Most small health centres enter almost every consultation. All four hospitals enter far fewer.

Malappuram district, Kerala. Share of patient visits where the doctor entered the consultation in the system. "Typical" means the middle value of all 65 health centres. Five centres were below 50%, two at zero. Source: eHealth Kerala, Malappuram district factsheet, February 2026.

Not every small centre is good: five of 65 were below 50%, and two had not entered a single consultation. But the typical health centre is near 100%, and every hospital is far below it. The same pattern shows up in Wayanad district: its General Hospital in Kalpetta is the district's lowest performer, with medicine orders entered for only 31% of patient visits, while most of Wayanad's health centres are between 80% and 95%.

The reports do not say why. Hospitals see more patients per doctor, have more departments and more handovers, and may run some work on other systems. But for patients, the result is the same: the place where the sickest patients go is where the record is thinnest.

Kerala's reports also show the weakest step. Only about a third of Malappuram's patient visits in early 2026 were linked to the patient's own hospital ID. Wayanad managed about 62%. If a visit is not linked to the patient's ID, the next doctor cannot easily find the last one.

This is one state and one month. Kerala is unusual because it publishes this at all. Most states could not show a chart like this even if they wanted to.

What Kerala does that other states can copy

Kerala is not perfect. Its own reports show weak hospitals and weak return-visit links. But it is the only state that shows its weaknesses every month, and it has built its system in a way other states can learn from. The project started with central government funding and went live from 2016–17. It now supports about 50,000 health staff, according to the national e-governance directory.

Figures as stated by Kerala's health minister and department, reported by Onmanorama (March 2023), Kerala Kaumudi, Deshabhimani and others (2023–2025). Points are evenly spaced, not to scale in time. Not checked against a single official series; counts may include medical college wings.

Three things stand out in that growth. The number of facilities doubled in under three years. The number of people with a permanent health ID grew almost eight times, from 0.34 crore in March 2023 to 2.63 crore by November 2025. And this second number matters most, because in Kerala a doctor can see your earlier visits only if you use your permanent ID, not a temporary token. That explains much of the weak return-visit link: in March 2023, about 3.2 crore people were registered, but only 10.6% had a permanent ID.

1

The state owns the software

The code belongs to the Kerala government, built on open-source tools, and the state says other states can ask to use it. No state is locked to one vendor.

2

Start with what saves staff time

The rollout began with OP registration, pharmacy, lab and the queue, the parts that remove daily drudgery. Doctors' clinical notes came later, once staff were used to the system.

3

A backup for when the internet fails

Each hospital has a small local server with a copy of its patients' data, so work continues when the network drops. Several northeastern states in our grid stall on exactly this problem.

4

Monthly public report cards

Every district gets a monthly factsheet with use rates for every facility, minimum activity thresholds, and a district ranking. Wayanad ranks second; Ernakulam, the state's commercial capital, ranks last.

5

A push for permanent IDs

From 0.34 crore to 2.63 crore permanent IDs in under three years, so that records can follow the person.

6

Standard medical codes

The system uses international coding standards for diagnoses and lab tests, so records can move between hospitals.

What Kerala still misses: its bigger hospitals lag well behind its health centres, as the Malappuram data shows. Online OP booking is still under 2% of visits in every district. Medical colleges are reported separately. And private hospitals, which the project once hoped to bring onto the same health cloud, are not part of the monthly reports. If India wants one state to learn from, Kerala is the best example available. Its own data says it is not finished.

Tamil Nadu: the record stops at the pharmacy

Tamil Nadu has run a hospital information system since 2007 and is moving to a new version, HMIS 3.0. Its own public health directorate studied the pilot in 13 primary health centres in Poonamallee, from February 2024 to March 2025. The setup was good: every centre had computers, tablets and broadband, every staff member was trained, and 54 of 56 reported software problems were fixed.

A missing pharmacy entry does not mean the patient did not get the medicine. It means the record does not show it. Source: Prabakaran J et al., "Formative evaluation of HMIS 3.0 pilot, Poonamallee HUD", Tamil Nadu Journal of Public Health and Medical Research, 2025.

So even with good infrastructure, the record lost 41% of the medicine trail between the doctor's desk and the pharmacy counter. The study also found ABHA IDs being created in only 3 of the 13 centres, and 7 of the 16 software modules still not switched on. The authors are clear: fix these gaps before scaling up statewide.

Delhi: the audit that a missing ICU bed started

Delhi's 38 government hospitals all have NextGen eHospital, built by NIC. In July 2026, the High Court asked NIC to check how it is really used. Two teams visited all 38 hospitals between 13 and 24 July. NIC told the court the modules were set up and training was ongoing, but use varied from hospital to hospital: 18 hospitals used more than ten modules, and 20 used five to ten. The court allowed a second surprise inspection to see if anything changed.

This is the most useful kind of evidence India has: someone walked in, unannounced, and checked. It happened only because a court asked.

Five states side by side: where the chain holds and breaks

Why is Kerala's data so much more useful than other states'? The answer is that its data is connected. The same state-owned system runs in every facility. The patient's permanent ID ties the visits together. The doctor's screen, the pharmacy and the lab sit in the same system, so an order and its result belong to one record. And every month, the state publishes how each facility uses it, then ranks districts on it.

Here is the same chain for the five states with the most public evidence. Each circle is one link. The line between two circles is only as strong as the weaker of the two.

"Not shown" means no public data was found, not that the link is missing on the ground. Bihar and Uttar Pradesh entries rely partly on state statements and dashboard flags.

Kerala's chain is the most complete, but even it breaks at the patient ID and the return visit. Tamil Nadu is strong at the doctor's desk but loses the medicine trail and publishes nothing regularly. Bihar has the best front door in India and almost no public evidence of what happens after it. Uttar Pradesh shows switches, not use. Delhi's chain became visible only because a court asked.

How any state can connect its chain

Kerala's experience and the gaps in the other states point to the same six steps. They work as a loop, not a one-time project: measure, publish, fix, measure again.

What about private hospitals?

Private hospitals treat a large share of India's patients, and many big chains run modern hospital software. But no private hospital group or industry body publishes how its systems are actually used, in the way Kerala does for government facilities. The one public signal points the other way: in October 2024, only 1.3% of ABHA Scan and Share registrations were at private facilities (Edition 79). So the gaps in this edition cannot be compared with private hospitals. They are simply not measured there.

Why no one can say how digital Indian hospitals are

Ask "what share of Indian hospitals are digital?" and you will get very different answers. Industry surveys published in 2026 disagree widely, because each defines "digital" differently and samples a different group of hospitals. One counts any hospital with billing software. Another counts only hospitals where doctors write notes on screen. National ABDM figures count health IDs and linked records, which is a different thing again.

All of these can be true at once. None of them tells you whether the next doctor sees your last prescription. That is the number India does not have.

Six measures that would show if it works

The states that publish use data, Kerala and Uttar Pradesh, show it can be done. These six measures, published every month by facility type, would let any state show real progress:

1

Consultations recorded

Doctor consultations entered in the system, out of all patient visits. Kerala already publishes this.

2

Medicines followed to the pharmacy

Prescriptions with medicines that have a dispensing entry. Tamil Nadu's pilot shows how to count it.

3

Lab results back to the doctor

Tests with a verified result in the patient's record, and the time it took.

4

Returning patients recognised

Visits linked to the patient's own ID, so the doctor can see the last visit.

5

Big hospitals reported separately

District hospitals, taluk hospitals and medical colleges shown on their own, not hidden in a state average.

6

Time saved, not added

Staff documentation minutes and patient waiting time, before and after. Gujarat's frontline study found 1.7 hours a day saved for each nurse; hospital systems should prove the same.

India has built the registration desk of digital health very well. The harder part starts after the patient sits down with the doctor. Until states publish what happens at the pharmacy, inside the district hospital and on the next visit, "digital" will keep meaning "registered".
